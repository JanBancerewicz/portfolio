## What is quantization?

You download a 12-billion-parameter model. The card says it is “open.” Your GPU has 16 GB. The loader asks for something closer to 27 GB and dies. However somehow a different filename ending in `q4_0` works flawlessly. Same model name, a quarter of the weight file. The work has a name.

<Term>Quantization</Term> means representing model values (usually weights, sometimes activations or the <Term>KV cache</Term>) with fewer bits via a <Term>scale</Term> and optional <Term>zero-point</Term>. Same architecture; smaller numerical ruler – not a smaller network.

That last sentence is the part people get wrong. A 4-bit checkpoint still has the same layers, attention, vocabulary, and parameter count as its 16-bit twin. Nothing was “distilled away.” You did not turn a 12B model into a 4B model. You changed how many distinct numbers each weight is allowed to take, and you stored a <Term>scale</Term> so those integers can be turned back into approximate floats when the runtime needs them.

### Why the tip exists: memory first

Local inference usually fails on memory before it fails on FLOPs. A rough lower bound for the weight footprint is:

```math
\text{memory} \approx \text{parameter\_count} \times \frac{\text{bits\_per\_parameter}}{8}
```

Ignore metadata, scratch buffers, the <Term>KV cache</Term>, and the runtime for a moment. A 12B model still needs about 24 GB of weight storage at 16 bits, and about 6 GB at 4 bits. Google’s Gemma 4 docs put the real loading numbers at **26.7 GB** in <Term>BF16</Term> and **6.7 GB** in <Term>Q4_0</Term>, including ~20% overhead – roughly a **75% cut in the static footprint**, not a 75% cut in intelligence. ([Gemma 4 model overview](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/core))

There is a second payoff once the model fits: generation rereads those weights token by token. Fewer bytes to move can mean higher tokens/s – *if* the runtime has efficient low-bit kernels. Hardware, batch size, and context length decide whether you feel that. Fitting at all is the first win; speed is a maybe.

### How the ruler works

So how do you actually put a float onto a smaller ruler? In **symmetric** quantization you pick a <Term>scale</Term> from the largest absolute value and map each value to an integer:

```math
\begin{aligned}
q &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}(x / s),\, q_{\min},\, q_{\max}\right) \\
\hat{x} &= s \cdot q
\end{aligned}
```

For signed N-bit integers, a common choice is:

```math
s = \frac{\max(|x|)}{2^{N-1} - 1}
```

Four bits give you 16 codes. Sixteen-bit float gives you a much wider dynamic range. The difference is the approximation error:

```math
e(x) = x - \hat{x}
```

You do not need every error to be zero. You need errors not to pile up where the task cares.

When values are not centered on zero, **asymmetric** quantization adds a <Term>zero-point</Term>:

```math
\begin{aligned}
q &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}(x / s) + z,\, q_{\min},\, q_{\max}\right) \\
\hat{x} &= s \cdot (q - z)
\end{aligned}
```

A shared <Term>scale</Term> and <Term>zero-point</Term> can cover a whole tensor, a channel, a token, or a small group – that is <Term>granularity</Term> / <Term>group-wise</Term> quantization. Finer scales usually preserve quality; they also cost more metadata and can be harder for kernels. These are the standard uniform formulas used across LLM papers, including <Term>SmoothQuant</Term> ([paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/xiao23c/xiao23c.pdf)).

A tiny example makes the failure mode obvious. Take:

```math
[-1.00,\, -0.62,\, -0.13,\, 0.22,\, 0.71,\, 1.00]
```

Signed 4-bit, max magnitude 1.00 → $s \approx 1/7 \approx 0.1429$. Then $0.71$ becomes:

```math
\begin{aligned}
q &= \mathrm{round}(0.71 / 0.1429) = 5 \\
\hat{x} &= 5 \times 0.1429 \approx 0.7145 \\
\text{error} &\approx 0.0045
\end{aligned}
```

Fine. Now replace one value with an <Term>outlier</Term> of `10.0` while the rest stay near zero. The <Term>scale</Term> jumps to cover that spike, and many ordinary values collapse onto the same few codes. That is why “float → int” is incomplete: distribution, <Term>granularity</Term>, clipping, and <Term>calibration</Term> – measuring real ranges on representative inputs – decide whether the smaller ruler still measures the parts of the model that matter.

## What you choose to shrink

Once the mapping is clear, the next question is *which* tensors get the smaller ruler.

- **Weight-only** (e.g. <Term>W4A16</Term>) – 4-bit weights, 16-bit activations. The usual local-LLM choice: weights dominate storage and the recipe is comparatively robust.
- **Weight + activation** (e.g. <Term>W8A8</Term>) – also low-bit activations. Can unlock integer matmul; activations are dynamic and often full of <Term>outlier</Term>s, so this is harder.
- **<Term>KV cache</Term> quantization** – shrinks the growing conversational state. Separate decision from quantizing the static weights you downloaded.

The letters are a contract: <Term>W4A16</Term> means 4-bit weights and 16-bit activations. A bare `Q4` in a filename is incomplete until you know format and kernel – which is why the Discord tip worked only because everyone already assumed <Term>GGUF</Term> + llama.cpp.

### When you apply it: after training or during

Two production paths, same goal.

**<Term>PTQ</Term>** (post-training quantization): train normally, convert afterward. Cheap. Aggressive conversion can hurt, especially with activations or coarse scales.

**<Term>QAT</Term>** (quantization-aware training): train while simulating rounding and clipping so weights adapt to the deployment constraint. Google’s Gemma 4 <Term>QAT</Term> checkpoints are built this way to keep quality while cutting memory. ([<Term>QAT</Term> announcement](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/quantization-aware-training-gemma-4/))

One naming trap: a <Term>QAT</Term> checkpoint may ship as half-precision weights for further conversion, as a quantized <Term>GGUF</Term> for llama.cpp / LM Studio, or as compressed tensors for vLLM / SGLang. “Unquantized <Term>QAT</Term>” does **not** mean “trained without quantization.” It means half-precision weights from a <Term>QAT</Term> pipeline.

## How you actually do it

Where the model runs picks the packaging. Local CPU / Apple Silicon / consumer GPU: a pre-quantized <Term>GGUF</Term> and a llama.cpp-compatible runtime is usually the least painful. Production GPU serving: use what the engine expects – Google routes Gemma <Term>QAT</Term> <Term>GGUF</Term> to llama.cpp / LM Studio and <Term>W4A16</Term> compressed tensors to vLLM / SGLang. ([Deployment routing](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/core))

A generic <Term>PTQ</Term> checklist, in the order you will actually care about:

1. Target representation (W8A16, <Term>W4A16</Term>, …).
2. <Term>granularity</Term> (per-tensor, per-channel, <Term>group-wise</Term>).
3. <Term>calibration</Term> data that looks like real prompts.
4. Measure ranges, pick scales, optionally clip outliers.
5. Quantize weights; keep <Term>scale</Term> metadata.
6. Export (<Term>GGUF</Term>, GPTQ, AWQ, compressed tensors, …).
7. Evaluate on standard benchmarks **and** your real prompts.
8. Measure peak memory, prefill, generation, and quality on the target box.

The core of symmetric weight quantization, as a teaching sketch:

```python
import torch

def quantize_symmetric(weights, bits=4):
    qmax = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1
    scale = weights.abs().amax().clamp_min(1e-8) / qmax
    quantized = torch.round(weights / scale).clamp(-qmax - 1, qmax)
    return quantized.to(torch.int8), scale

def dequantize(quantized, scale):
    return quantized.float() * scale
```

Real formats pack multiple low-bit values into bytes, store per-group scales, and need optimized kernels. A mathematically correct conversion can still be slow if the runtime unpacks inefficiently – which brings us back to why “it fits” and “it is fast” are different claims.

### Gemma 4 puts numbers on the tip

Dense Gemma 4 12B instruction-tuned is a clean case. Baseline: `google/gemma-4-12B-it` in <Term>BF16</Term>. Quantized path: official <Term>QAT</Term> <Term>Q4_0</Term>, typically as <Term>GGUF</Term> (`google/gemma-4-12B-it-qat-q4_0-gguf`).

| Representation | Bits per weight | Approx. memory to load | Relative to BF16 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| BF16 baseline | 16 | 26.7 GB | 100% |
| SFP8 | 8 | 13.4 GB | 50% |
| Q4_0 | 4 | 6.7 GB | 25% |

Google’s figures include ~20% overhead. <Term>Q4_0</Term> saves ~20 GB vs <Term>BF16</Term>. Context, <Term>KV cache</Term>, framework overhead, and multimodal inputs sit **on top** – a “6.7 GB model” does not mean a machine with exactly 6.7 GB of VRAM will run it happily. ([Memory requirements](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/core))

Parameter count alone is incomplete in another way too. Gemma 4 includes a 26B A4B MoE that activates ~4B parameters per token, but **all** experts still load for fast routing. <Term>Quantization</Term> shrinks the storage cost of those weights; it does not make inactive experts disappear. Same lesson as the opening: smaller ruler, not fewer layers.

### Quality is a claim you have to earn

There is no universal “quantization accuracy penalty.” Bit width, quantizer, <Term>calibration</Term>, group size, runtime, and task all move the needle. A 4-bit model can look identical to baseline on one workload and stumble on precise arithmetic, long reasoning chains, or rare tokens.

For Gemma 4 specifically: Google publishes family capability scores, but the public card does **not** give a controlled <Term>BF16</Term>-vs-<Term>Q4_0</Term> table. <Term>QAT</Term> is described as staying close to <Term>BF16</Term>; the docs give hard memory numbers. That supports a deployment claim about memory. It does not license inventing a quality delta.

| Question | BF16 Gemma 4 12B | Official QAT Q4_0 Gemma 4 12B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weight precision | 16-bit | 4-bit weights, Q4_0 |
| Static load memory | 26.7 GB | 6.7 GB |
| Memory reduction | Baseline | ~75% |
| Expected quality | Reference | Designed to stay close to BF16 – verify on your task |
| Best fit | Large / multi-GPU | Local GPU, CPU, laptop, constrained server |
| Published same-run quality score | Reference | Not in the official card |

Resist filling that last row with a number from a different runtime, prompt template, revision, or recipe. Useful as engineering notes; not a controlled comparison.

To earn your own numbers, keep everything except precision fixed:

```bash
# Matching chat templates and the same prompt set.
llama-bench -m gemma-4-12b-it-bf16.gguf -p 512 -n 128
llama-bench -m gemma-4-12b-it-qat-q4_0.gguf -p 512 -n 128
```

Record prefill throughput, generation throughput, peak RSS, time-to-first-token, and quality on a fixed eval set. Report model revision, quant format, runtime version, hardware, context, batch size, sampling, and dataset – or “Q4 is 2× faster” is just another Discord tip without receipts.

## When it changes what can run at all

<Term>Quantization</Term> matters most when it changes the set of machines that can host the model. Google’s Gemma 4 estimates put 31B at **69.9 GB** <Term>BF16</Term> vs **17.5 GB** <Term>Q4_0</Term> – multi-GPU vs something that can fit a high-end 24 GB card before context and runtime. ([Model overview](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/core))

Speed stays less predictable: good kernels and less traffic help; bad unpack paths or prefill-dominated workloads do not. Energy and cost follow the systems story – fewer GPUs, lower-power devices, more concurrent requests – not promises glued to the string `Q4` in a filename.

Spend bits where they protect quality; remove them where they mostly encode redundant detail. The best quantized model is not the one with the fewest bits – it is the one that meets quality, latency, memory, and cost for the job you opened Discord about.

### Takeaways

1. Same architecture, smaller ruler – that is the definition, and it is enough to kill the “smaller model” myth.
2. Memory is why the tip exists; kernels decide whether speed follows.
3. <Term>outlier</Term>s, <Term>calibration</Term>, and <Term>granularity</Term> decide whether the tip still answers well.
4. Gemma 4 makes the payoff concrete: **26.7 GB <Term>BF16</Term> → 6.7 GB <Term>Q4_0</Term>** for 12B. Measure quality yourself; do not invent the delta Google did not publish.
